Two Screenings, Ten Years Apart

In 2015, Bophana Center gave one night to a documentary about a poet deported to a country he'd never lived in. In 2026, Meta House gave one night to five Cambodian filmmakers' first films. Different rooms, same instinct.

Archival Record

We went looking through paperback's old Facebook posts for this site's rebuild, and one from July 2015 stopped us: a one-night, Japanese-subtitled screening of a documentary called Cambodian Son, at a venue called Bophana Center, twenty minutes' walk from where Anti‑Archive's short film night would happen at Meta House eleven years later. We hadn't set out to write about Cambodian cinema twice. The city, it turns out, had already made that decision for us.

Cambodian Son is a 2014 documentary, directed by the Japan-born, Cambodia-based filmmaker Masahiro Sugano, about the poet Kosal Khiev. Khiev was born in 1980 in the Khao-I-Dang refugee camp on the Thai border and grew up in Santa Ana, California. At sixteen he was convicted of attempted murder and served fourteen years, including eighteen months in solitary confinement at New Folsom State Prison, where he began writing poetry. In 2011, on release, the United States deported him to Cambodia — a country he had never lived in.

In Phnom Penh he met Sugano and the performance artist Anida Yoeu Ali, who together run the independent media lab Studio Revolt, founded in the city in 2011. The following year, selected from six thousand nominees, Khiev represented Cambodia as a poet at the London 2012 Cultural Olympiad; his filmed performance Why I Write later won Best Poem Performance on Film at the Berlin Zebra Poetry Film Festival. Cambodian Son follows him through that period — between a family in California he's barred from visiting and a country he's required to call home.

Both nights gave a room to someone working out, in public, what a life adds up to when the place you're from and the place you're required to be aren't the same place.

Bophana Center, where the 2015 screening was held, was co-founded in 2006 by the filmmaker Rithy Panh and Ieu Pannakar as an audiovisual archive for Cambodian film heritage — newsreels, royal cinema, documentaries, anything worth not losing. It's still standing, still screening. Meta House, where Anti‑Archive's programme played in 2026, opened a year later, in 2007, on the same premise from a different angle: give the city's independent artists a room. Between them they've spent close to forty combined years doing a fairly unglamorous job — not making the films, just making sure there's somewhere in Phnom Penh to show them.

Two nights

2015
Cambodian Son Bophana Center · one-night screening, Japanese subtitles

A documentary about a single deported poet, shown once, to whoever in Phnom Penh could read the subtitles and wanted to sit with the question it was asking.

2026
First Films at Meta House Meta House · Anti-Archive Short Film Night

Five first or early films by Cambodian directors, shown once, mostly about the distance between what a family expects and what a person decides for themselves.

We're not going to claim Phnom Penh's screening rooms are running some coordinated decade-long programme. They aren't. But when a French colonial-era archive center and a German-Cambodian cultural house, working eleven years and a few kilometres apart, both end up giving a single night to someone's account of not quite belonging where they're told to — that's not nothing either. It's closer to what these two buildings were built to do in the first place.

Cambodian Son isn't streaming anywhere we could find. Ask Bophana Center; archives are, after all, the whole point of the building.
